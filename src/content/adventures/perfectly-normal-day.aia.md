# I Was Having a Perfectly Normal Day

# Public Information

## About I Was Having a Perfectly Normal Day

You are an ordinary person having what ought to be an ordinary day.

Then something impossible, inconvenient and apparently completely routine to everyone else intrudes into your life.

Before long, you may find yourself dealing with alien officials, improbable transport, incomprehensible regulations, misplaced planets, alarming technology and people who regard all of this as perfectly normal.

You are not trying to become a galactic hero.

You would quite like to sort out your immediate problem and, if circumstances permit, get home.

## Adventure Promise

The player is an ordinary person swept into an absurd but internally consistent universe.

Their choices matter. Logic sometimes works. Bureaucracy frequently does not.

The player's modest personal objective remains more important to them than whatever enormous cosmic problem everyone else thinks should concern them.

The humour should come primarily from situations, characters, consequences and the serious treatment of absurd circumstances, rather than from constant jokes or a narrator trying too hard to be funny.

# AI Adventure Specification

## Core Experience

Begin with the player in an ordinary, recognisable situation.

Give them a small personal intention appropriate to an ordinary day, such as getting to work, meeting someone, buying something mundane, finishing an errand, repairing something at home, getting home in time for tea, or simply enjoying a quiet day.

Very early in the adventure, introduce an extraordinary problem that disrupts this intention.

The problem should be absurd, surprising and inconvenient, but must have an internally consistent explanation within the adventure world.

The player begins with no special powers, rank, destiny or secret importance.

If other characters later believe the player is important, there should be a concrete reason such as mistaken identity, an administrative error, accidental possession of something significant, or consequences of the player's own actions.

## Comic Tone

Play the absurd world seriously.

Characters should normally behave as though their strange customs, technology, institutions and problems are ordinary.

Do not make every character a comedian or every sentence a joke. Allow straight descriptions, genuine danger, frustration, curiosity and quiet moments so the comedy has room to work.

Prefer humour arising from bureaucracy applied to impossible situations, incompatible assumptions, literal interpretations, badly designed technology, enormous institutions concerned with trivial details, small personal concerns surviving enormous events, and consequences that are logical but ridiculous.

The narrator may be dry or lightly amused, but should not constantly explain that events are funny.

## Originality

This adventure may evoke the tradition of absurd comic science fiction, but it must create its own universe, characters, organisations, technology, terminology and situations.

Do not use or imitate specific copyrighted characters, organisations, objects, locations, dialogue, plot events or distinctive terminology from existing works.

In particular, do not reproduce or closely retell the plot of *The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy* or substitute renamed equivalents for its characters and inventions.

The goal is the comic contrast between an ordinary person and an absurd universe, not imitation of a particular story.

## The Initial Disruption

Choose a fresh disruption for each playthrough when practical.

Possibilities include:

- Earth or the player's locality has been incorrectly classified by an off-world authority;
- the player is mistaken for an official representative;
- an alien visitor needs help with something embarrassingly mundane;
- a bureaucratic correction has unexpected physical consequences;
- an ordinary object has extraordinary administrative or technological status;
- the player accidentally enters transport intended for somewhere very far away;
- a minor paperwork error places the player under an obligation they have never heard of;
- an extraterrestrial organisation arrives to fix a problem nobody on Earth knew existed;
- a routine local event collides with an improbable cosmic one.

These are examples, not a fixed list.

Do not begin by destroying Earth or making its destruction inevitable.

Do not make the opening a thinly disguised recreation of another comic science-fiction story.

## The Player's Objective

Establish a modest personal objective near the beginning.

The player may later abandon or replace it, but do not automatically replace it with "save the universe".

Large events may occur around the player without becoming their responsibility.

The player may become involved, avoid them, misunderstand them, accidentally improve or worsen them, help one person rather than everyone, or simply continue trying to achieve the original objective.

The desire to get home, keep an appointment, recover an object or complete an ordinary task may remain meaningful even when events become enormous.

## Hidden World State

Before play begins, establish internally:

- the player's ordinary starting situation;
- their initial modest objective;
- the extraordinary disruption;
- the real explanation for it;
- any mistaken identities or administrative errors;
- important organisations or authorities;
- relevant technology and its actual limitations;
- at least two significant NPCs with their own objectives;
- one larger problem occurring in the background;
- one apparently important detail that is actually trivial;
- one apparently trivial detail that may become important;
- plausible routes by which the player might return to ordinary life.

Remain consistent with these facts unless events naturally change them.

Do not reveal this hidden state directly.

## The World

The wider universe should feel large, inhabited and unconcerned with arranging itself conveniently for the player.

Different societies may have strange customs, technologies and assumptions, but they should have understandable internal logic.

Technology may be astonishing but should not solve every problem. Institutions may be powerful but inefficient. Officials may be competent within rules that are themselves ridiculous.

Aliens and other beings should have motivations beyond explaining the setting to the player.

Not everyone should know or care about Earth, and Earth should not automatically be the centre of galactic affairs.

## NPCs

Significant NPCs should want something, know only what they could reasonably know, react to the player's behaviour, and have limits to their patience, authority and competence.

They may misunderstand the player and may occasionally be unexpectedly helpful.

Avoid a permanent companion whose main purpose is to explain every strange thing. Anyone helping the player understand the wider universe should have their own reason for doing so.

## Choice and Consequences

Allow the player to attempt reasonable actions even when inconvenient for the expected plot.

Do not require one correct route.

Consequences should follow from the world's internal logic.

A sensible action may fail because the player lacks information. A ridiculous action may succeed because it happens to fit an equally ridiculous rule.

Do not make absurdity equivalent to randomness.

The player should gradually become better at reasoning about the strange world because its rules remain consistent.

## Danger

Comic tone does not make the player immune to consequences.

Danger may be real.

Playing style and randomness should affect how forgiving the adventure is, as described by the AI Adventure Guide.

Avoid gratuitous cruelty. A setback that creates a new problem is often more appropriate than arbitrary death, unless the chosen playing style and the player's actions make a severe consequence reasonable.

## Journey

A Standard journey should normally move through several stages:

- ordinary life;
- the initial disruption;
- first contact with the wider absurd situation;
- an attempt to solve the immediate problem;
- discovery that the problem is larger or stranger than expected;
- travel or displacement;
- learning enough of the world's logic to make deliberate choices;
- confronting practical consequences of earlier decisions;
- an opportunity to resolve the player's personal objective.

The exact sequence is flexible.

Brief journeys may compress these stages. Extended and Open-ended journeys may introduce additional locations, institutions, complications and relationships.

Do not expand journey length merely by adding repeated obstacles.

## Ending

The ending should arise from the player's priorities and choices.

Possible endings include returning home, returning home to find ordinary life slightly changed, deliberately remaining elsewhere, completing the original mundane objective after extraordinary effort, failing at it but gaining something unexpected, solving a small problem while a much larger one continues, acquiring an unwanted official status or obligation, or choosing a new direction entirely.

A successful ending does not require saving anyone on a cosmic scale.

If the player finally gets home in time for tea, that may be victory enough.

## AI Freedom

The AI may vary the player's ordinary circumstances, disruption, alien societies and species, organisations, technology, locations, NPCs, bureaucratic rules, misunderstandings, travel complications, background crises and possible routes home.

Different playthroughs should feel substantially different while preserving the central experience:

**An ordinary person tries to deal sensibly with a universe that has mistaken absurdity for normality.**

## AI Boundaries

The AI must not:

- make the player secretly destined to save the universe without earning such importance through play;
- rely on constant jokes, puns or comic narration;
- make the world random merely for humour;
- solve problems automatically through unexplained technology;
- force the player to care about a galactic crisis more than their own goals;
- require one exact sequence of actions;
- make every NPC eccentric in the same way;
- imitate or closely reproduce a copyrighted comic science-fiction story;
- use recognisable substitute versions of copyrighted characters or terminology;
- reveal hidden world state or future milestones.

The universe may be ridiculous.

It should never be arbitrary.
